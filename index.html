What’s a World Model Without People in It?

Toward Correct Social World Models

Bringing together researchers in multi-agent systems, human behavior simulation, social cognition, and social robotics to shape the foundations of social world modeling.

01 · Overview

The world doesn’t evolve through physics alone.

What does it mean to model the world if we leave out one of its strongest driving forces: people? Much of the recent discussion around world models has focused on representing environments, predicting physical dynamics, and enabling agents to plan and act within them. But people change the world, and one another, through their intentions, goals, beliefs, norms, relationships, and actions.

Across multi-agent systems, social simulation, theory of mind, computational social science, and social robotics, researchers have begun addressing pieces of this problem from different angles: language-agent research studies interaction and coordination; human simulation models individual and collective behavior; theory-of-mind research examines beliefs and perspectives; and social robotics investigates how embodied agents understand and respond to people. These efforts point toward a common problem, but have largely developed in parallel, with different abstractions, terminology, assumptions, and standards of evaluation.

This workshop is a meeting point for these communities to compare approaches, develop a shared technical language, and shape the emerging field of social world models, organized around three shared pillars.

Pillar 1

Representation

What is the state of a social world?

Pillar 2

Dynamics

How do social worlds change?

Pillar 3

Evaluation

What makes a social world model correct?

Learning and empirical grounding cut across all three.

I

Clarify what social world models represent and predict

Compare how different communities model people, mental states, relationships, norms, and social change across individuals, interactions, groups, and populations.

II

Connect research across computational and embodied settings

Identify transferable methods and shared challenges in language agents, human behavior simulation, multi-agent systems, and human–robot interaction.

III

Develop an evaluation agenda grounded in evidence

Clarify what makes a social world model correct, which evidence supports which claims, and where current benchmarks leave important gaps.

02 · Topics

Topics of interest

We welcome methodological, empirical, theoretical, and position papers on topics including, but not limited to:

Representation

What is the state of a social world?

Beliefs, goals, intentions, preferences, memory, relationships, roles, norms, culture, and information state, including who knows what; explicit versus latent representations; partial observability, information asymmetry, and individual heterogeneity.

Dynamics

How do social worlds change?

Communication, belief and preference change, social influence, negotiation, coordination, competition, and relationship formation; connections between individual behavior, group interaction, and population dynamics over multiple timescales.

Evaluation

What makes a social world model correct?

Construct and predictive validity, distributional fidelity, longitudinal consistency, counterfactual and interactive validity, and downstream utility; benchmarks that distinguish plausible behavior from faithful social simulation.

Learning & Empirical Grounding

What data and evidence ground the model?

Observational and experimental data, surveys, behavioral traces, synthetic populations, multimodal and embodied data, longitudinal observations, cultural and demographic coverage, privacy, and simulation-to-human transfer.

Social World Models for Agents & Robots

How do applications test or inform the three pillars?

Human and user simulators, interactive planning, socially aware agents, collaborative multi-agent systems, assistive robotics, and long-term human–robot interaction.

03 · Submit

Call for papers

We solicit non-archival, anonymized submissions through OpenReview in two formats. Our call prioritizes new and ongoing work rather than republication of conference papers.

6pages

Regular papers

Original or ongoing research.

2pages

Tiny / short papers

Focused ideas, late-breaking results, negative results, benchmark critiques, re-analyses, or position statements.

  • Page limits refer to main text and exclude references and appendices.
  • Each submission receives at least two double-blind reviews from reviewers with relevant expertise.
  • Acceptance considers technical or conceptual quality, relevance, clarity, and potential to advance workshop discussion.
  • We follow the host conference’s author AI usage policy. Tiny/short submissions must be primarily human-authored; AI-generated papers are not permitted in this track.
  • Accepted papers will be public on OpenReview and this website.
